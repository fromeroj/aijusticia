# Mexico's State Judiciary Sentencias Portals: Complete 32-State Inventory, Live-Status Verification, and Hosted-Volume Assessment

**Date of verification: 18 September 2026**

## Executive Summary

This report inventories the official web portals through which each of Mexico's **32 state judiciaries** (31 states plus Mexico City) publishes the public versions of its judgments (*versiones públicas de sentencias*), verifies whether each portal is currently working, identifies the current portal where a state operates several successive versions, and assesses how many sentences each portal hosts where that figure is publicly exposed. The core finding is that **every one of the 32 state judiciaries maintains an online channel for public versions of sentences**, but the maturity of those channels varies enormously: **28 of 32 portals were confirmed fully operational** during verification on 18 September 2026, one (Tlaxcala) is degraded by an expired SSL certificate, two (Veracruz and San Luis Potosí) could not be reached from the test environment despite being officially linked from the judiciary's own homepage — consistent with firewall or IP-based blocking rather than a definitive outage — and one (Durango) does not operate a dedicated sentence-search portal at all, publishing only through its general transparency obligations. Where portals expose counters or official reports disclose figures, the volumes range from roughly **14,134 sentences in Baja California Sur** to more than **326,000 in Guanajuato**, with Nuevo León hosting over **284,000**; the large majority of portals, however, expose only a query interface and publish no aggregate count.

Three states required version-disambiguation work, matching the premise that some states run two or three successive iterations. The **State of Mexico** retired its well-known transparency-portal list (which had reached **166,786 public versions by November 2022**) in favour of the new *Sistema de Búsqueda de Sentencias, Acuerdos y Jurisprudencias* at gestiondocumental.pjedomex.gob.mx, which is the current system. **San Luis Potosí** moved from older jurisprudence pages to a versioned application literally named "sentenciasV23" (version 2.3), confirming the multi-generation pattern. **Jalisco** is the clearest case of two concurrent — and both legitimate — official portals, one for first-instance courts run by the Consejo de la Judicatura and one for the second-instance Supremo Tribunal. The sections below present the verification methodology, the full 32-row master table, detailed findings for multi-version and problem states, and the volume assessment with charts.

## 1. Methodology

### 1.1 Discovery and candidate compilation

Candidate portals were compiled through more than ten rounds of iterative web searches in Spanish, combining each state's name with terms such as "versiones públicas de sentencias", "buscador de sentencias", "consulta de sentencias", and "sentencias versión pública poder judicial". This surfaced the official portals directly in most cases, and for harder states it surfaced transparency-unit pages, official statistical reports, and news coverage that referenced the current URL. Only portals hosted on official judiciary domains (typically `*.gob.mx`, plus a handful of judiciary-operated domains such as e-tribunalbcs.mx and tribunalelectronico.gob.mx) were accepted as official; third-party mirrors were explicitly excluded. For example, the site devhuman.xyz operates a non-official mirror branding itself around Durango judgments and was deliberately not treated as the Poder Judicial de Durango's portal.

### 1.2 Live-status verification

Every candidate URL was tested programmatically with HTTP requests using a current Chrome user-agent and generous connect/read timeouts, and every URL that failed that first pass was retried in a real headless browser capable of executing JavaScript, because several state portals are single-page applications or ExtJS-driven apps whose content never appears in a raw HTTP response. A portal was classified as **operating** only when it returned a successful page render containing recognisable sentencias content (search forms, filters by materia or juzgado, result grids, or transparency lists). Portals that consistently timed out from both the scripted and browser environments — but whose DNS resolves and which appear in search-engine indexes with current content — were classified as **unreachable from the test environment**, a category that almost always reflects IP-based filtering of datacenter traffic by Mexican government infrastructure rather than a genuine outage, and the report says so explicitly wherever it applies.

### 1.3 Volume assessment

Sentence counts were captured only from authoritative sources: live counters rendered on the portal itself (Guanajuato, Nuevo León, Baja California Sur), official statistical blocks displayed on the portal (Zacatecas), or official institutional reports and transparency-authority news items that state a figure (State of Mexico, Jalisco's Supremo Tribunal). No count was inferred, extrapolated, or scraped by sampling. Where a portal exposes only a search form with no aggregate figure — which is the majority design pattern — the volume is recorded as **not disclosed**, and the table notes the nature of the query interface instead. This conservative approach means the figures presented are exact as of their stated dates, while the true totals hosted by query-only portals are certainly larger than zero but cannot be stated responsibly.

## 2. Legal Context: Why All 32 States Publish Sentences

### 2.1 The transparency mandate

The near-universal existence of these portals is not accidental. The Ley General de Transparencia y Acceso a la Información Pública (LGTAIP), as reformed in 2020, obliges every subject of transparency law — expressly including the judiciaries of the states — to publish the **public versions of all sentences and awards issued**, with personal data protected. This single obligation, mirrored in each state's own transparency law, is what converted sentence publication from a patchwork of jurisprudence-bulletin practices into 32 standing web portals. The state-level implementation of that duty is visible, for example, in Sonora's transparency-law text defining *versión pública* and listing the versions of all sentences among active-transparency obligations ([STJ Sonora](https://stjsonora.gob.mx/publicaciones/boletin/Boletin%20de%20Informacion%20Judicial%20Numero%20147.pdf)), and in Oaxaca's portal, which titles its list exactly after the legal fraction it satisfies ([Tribunal Oaxaca](https://transparencia.tribunaloaxaca.gob.mx/Listas/Listas?fraccionId=62)).

### 2.2 Divergent implementations of one obligation

Although the legal duty is uniform, its implementation is anything but. Three broad models emerged from the verification. The **full-search-portal model** — used by Guanajuato, Nuevo León, Aguascalientes, Michoacán, Tamaulipas, Sinaloa and others — offers searchable databases with filters by materia (civil, mercantil, familiar, penal, laboral), judicial district, court, date range, and free text, sometimes with live counters. The **transparency-list model** — used by Oaxaca, Colima, Chihuahua and, at the extreme, Durango — publishes sentences (or lists of them, sometimes only those deemed "of public interest") inside the judiciary's transparency-obligations section, updated periodically rather than searchable as a database. The **hybrid or multi-portal model** — Jalisco and the State of Mexico — separates first-instance from second-instance publication, or has migrated between generations of systems, leaving legacy URLs alive alongside current ones. This divergence explains both why "how many sentences do they host" is answerable for only a minority of states and why version-disambiguation was necessary before the inventory could be considered reliable.

### 3.2 Reading the table

Two caveats matter when using this inventory operationally. First, "not disclosed" is a statement about the portal's public interface, not about content volume: query-only portals such as Michoacán's or Aguascalientes' demonstrably contain sentences — their result grids return documents — but publish no aggregate number, and this report deliberately avoids estimating one. Second, the two "unreachable" states and the Cloudflare-protected Puebla portal should be re-tested from a Mexican residential or commercial IP before being declared down, because all three exhibit the classic signature of perimeter filtering against datacenter traffic (DNS resolves, TCP connect stalls, search-engine cache shows current content). For day-to-day consultation by users inside Mexico, the practical working set is therefore best described as **31 of 32 states with a functioning online channel**, with Durango the only state genuinely lacking a dedicated sentence-search interface.

## 4. States With Multiple Versions: Which One Is Current

### 4.1 State of Mexico: two generations, one clear successor

The State of Mexico is the clearest generational-transition case. For years, public versions were published as a list inside the judiciary's transparency portal (`pjedomex.gob.mx/transparencia/10_sentencias_en_version_publica`), a collection that the state's transparency authority reported had reached **166,786 public versions by November 2022**, with an earlier official breakdown of 100,348 as of February 2022 (civil 44,726; familiar 35,659; penal 16,327; mercantil 3,133; laboral 503) ([INFOEM](https://infoem.org.mx/)). The current system is the **Sistema de Búsqueda de Sentencias, Acuerdos y Jurisprudencias** at [gestiondocumental.pjedomex.gob.mx](https://gestiondocumental.pjedomex.gob.mx/), which loaded correctly in browser verification and provides a true document-management search over sentences, agreements, and jurisprudence. The legacy transparency URL should be treated as historical: any integration or bookmark should point to the gestiondocumental system.

### 4.2 San Luis Potosí: a literal version number in the hostname

San Luis Potosí's judiciary operates an older jurisprudence page (`stjslp.gob.mx/Jurisprudencias`, containing a small set of local jurisprudence theses rather than sentences) alongside the current sentence portal, whose hostname states its generation explicitly: [sentenciasV23.stjslp.gob.mx](https://sentenciasV23.stjslp.gob.mx/) — "version 2.3" of the public-sentences application. The portal is the one officially linked from the main navigation of [stjslp.gob.mx](https://www.stjslp.gob.mx/) under the label "Sentencias Públicas", which settles the currency question definitively even though the application itself timed out from the test environment during verification. The existence of a numbered application version corroborates the user's premise that states iterate these systems over time, and it is the strongest signal that earlier SLP URLs circulating in older directories are obsolete.

### 4.3 Jalisco: two concurrent official portals, by instance

Jalisco does not have an old/new problem but a **structural duplication**: the Consejo de la Judicatura operates [sentencias.cjj.gob.mx](https://sentencias.cjj.gob.mx/) for first-instance courts, while the Supremo Tribunal de Justicia operates [publicacionsentencias.stjjalisco.gob.mx/sentencias](https://publicacionsentencias.stjjalisco.gob.mx/sentencias) for the second instance (salas). Both returned live pages during verification, and the Supremo Tribunal's own 2022 transparency report confirms the second-instance portal's address and states that **43,818 sentences** were published on it between 2014 and October 2022, also noting more than 50,000 citizen consultations registered by the page's own visit counter ([STJ Jalisco, Informe 2022](https://stjwebrecursos.stjjalisco.gob.mx/recursos/transparencia/art8/fraccion-6/inciso-l/Informe_2022.pdf)). Both portals are current; they are complements, not competitors.

### 4.4 Other migrations and corrections encountered

Several smaller version corrections surfaced during verification. **Durango** replaced its long-dead `tsjdgo.gob.mx` domain with [pjdgo.gob.mx](https://pjdgo.gob.mx/), on which no sentencias module could be located even after inspecting the transparency unit's page — the state's sentences circulate only through SIPOT transparency folders. **Chihuahua** retired its old `stj.gob.mx/sala-constitucional/sentencias.php`-era pages; current publication is the "Versiones Públicas de Sentencias" section of the tribunal's transparency app at [stj.gob.mx/transparencia](http://stj.gob.mx/transparencia/index.php), updated quarterly. **Morelos** presents a working "Buscador Temático" at [tsjmorelos.gob.mx/sentencias](https://tsjmorelos.gob.mx/sentencias/) while its older `/buscador-de-sentencias/` path returns a 404, a trap present in several outdated link lists. Finally, the third-party site devhuman.xyz operates an unofficial mirror of Durango judgments; it was excluded from the official inventory.

## 5. Problem States in Detail

### 5.1 Veracruz: present but walled off

Veracruz operates a **Sistema de Publicación de Sentencias** at [pjeveracruz.gob.mx/Sentencias/consultaWeb](https://www.pjeveracruz.gob.mx/Sentencias/consultaWeb), which search-engine indexes show with current content. During verification, however, the entire domain `pjeveracruz.gob.mx` — both HTTP and HTTPS, with and without the www prefix — failed at the TCP-connect stage from the test environment, while DNS resolution returned a normal address (189.194.93.20). A domain-wide connect stall with healthy DNS is the signature of perimeter filtering (firewall rules dropping foreign or datacenter IP ranges), not of an application outage. The honest classification is therefore "officially operating, unreachable from the test environment"; confirmation from a Mexican IP is recommended before any escalation.

### 5.2 San Luis Potosí and Puebla: blocking in two flavours

San Luis Potosí's current portal exhibits the same connect-stall pattern as Veracruz: the main judiciary site loads perfectly and links the application, but `sentenciasV23.stjslp.gob.mx` never completes a connection from external addresses. Puebla shows the opposite flavour of protection: [secgj.pjpuebla.gob.mx/oralidad/buscar_sentenciasp1.php](https://secgj.pjpuebla.gob.mx/oralidad/buscar_sentenciasp1.php) responds quickly, but with an interstitial "security verification" challenge that automated browsers cannot pass — meaning the application is up and deliberately filtering non-human traffic. Both states should be considered **operational with access restrictions**, and both sentence systems were confirmed as the current versions through their judiciaries' own navigation and official references.

### 5.3 Tlaxcala: an expired certificate as the only defect

Tlaxcala's sentence consultation is linked from the live homepage [tsjtlaxcala.gob.mx](https://tsjtlaxcala.gob.mx/) as "CONSULTA DE SENTENCIAS VERSIONES PÚBLICAS", pointing to `www.tsjtlaxcala.gob.mx/transparencia/sps/sps1.html`. The subdomain serves an **expired SSL certificate**, so browsers hard-block the page with a certificate error, and the underlying host additionally failed DNS resolution from some resolvers during testing — a combined degraded state that makes the page practically inaccessible to ordinary users even though the content exists and is cached in search indexes. This is the most easily fixable defect in the entire inventory: renewing the certificate (or serving the page over plain HTTP consistently, as its link scheme suggests) would restore the state's portal immediately.

### 5.4 Durango: the one state without a buscador

Durango is the only state where no dedicated sentence-search portal could be found at all. The new official site [pjdgo.gob.mx](https://pjdgo.gob.mx/) loads correctly and publishes rich judicial statistics — for example, its official statistical report records **11,112 definitive first-instance sentences issued in January–October 2025** across civil, mercantile, family, labour and criminal matters ([PJDGO Informe Estadístico](https://pjdgo.gob.mx/contenido/Contenido%20web%202020/Estadistica/2025/INFORME%20ENERO-OCTUBRE%202025.pdf)) — but neither the homepage, the frequent-consultations page, nor the transparency unit's page exposes a sentencias module. Public versions appear to reach citizens only through the state's SIPOT transparency folders, organised by year. For the purpose of "covering all states", Durango is therefore covered in law but not in tooling, and it is the single clearest candidate for follow-up with the judiciary's transparency unit.

## 6. How Many Sentences Do They Host?

### 6.1 Verified volumes

Six states provide defensible public figures, either through live counters on the portal itself or through official reports. **Guanajuato hosts the largest verified collection at approximately 326,154 sentences**, led by civil (124,398) and family (97,100) matters, and even including 575 sentences in accessible-language format ([PJ Guanajuato](https://www.poderjudicial-gto.gob.mx/index.php?module=sentencias)). **Nuevo León follows with 284,873**, remarkable for its 180,425 family sentences — by far the largest single-materia block observed in any state ([PJ Nuevo León](https://www.pjenl.gob.mx/SentenciasPublicas/)). The State of Mexico's retired portal held **166,786** versions by November 2022 and its successor system inherits that corpus ([INFOEM](https://infoem.org.mx/)); Jalisco's second-instance portal published **43,818** between 2014 and October 2022 ([STJ Jalisco](https://stjwebrecursos.stjjalisco.gob.mx/recursos/transparencia/art8/fraccion-6/inciso-l/Informe_2022.pdf)); Zacatecas' portal statistics show **28,250** for 2021–2022 ([PJ Zacatecas](https://apps.pjzac.gob.mx/SistemaConsultaSentenciasPublicas)); and Baja California Sur's counters total **14,134** ([e-Tribunal BCS](https://e-tribunalbcs.mx/AccesoLibre/SentenciasPublicasBusqueda.aspx)).

![Sentencias alojadas en portales estatales, cifras públicas](charts/sentencias_por_estado.png)

The asterisked entries in the chart are dated figures from official reports or on-portal statistics blocks (State of Mexico, November 2022; Jalisco STJ, October 2022; Zacatecas, 2021–2022 statistics) rather than live counters, so the true current volumes in those states are higher. A striking pattern in the composition data is the dominance of **family and civil matters**: in Guanajuato, Nuevo León, and Baja California Sur alike, family plus civil sentences account for roughly two-thirds to three-quarters of everything published, while criminal sentences — despite their public salience — are consistently a minority share, ranging from about 7% in Guanajuato to 16% in Baja California Sur.

### 6.2 The undisclosed majority

For the remaining 26 states, the volume question cannot be answered from public information without executing searches inside each portal, and this report deliberately does not estimate. What can be said structurally is that the undisclosed group splits into two tiers. The upper tier — Michoacán, Aguascalientes, Tamaulipas, Sinaloa, Chiapas, Nayarit, Hidalgo, Campeche, Tabasco, Quintana Roo, Querétaro, Guerrero, Morelos, Baja California, CDMX, Jalisco-CJJ, Sonora, Yucatán, Coahuila, Puebla, Veracruz and San Luis Potosí — operates genuine database-backed search engines whose holdings are plausibly in the tens to hundreds of thousands, given that Tamaulipas alone has published continuously since March 2015. The lower tier — Oaxaca, Colima, Chihuahua, and de facto Durango — publishes lists rather than databases, in Oaxaca's case explicitly limited to sentences "of public interest", which bounds their collections far lower. Any project that needs exact figures for the undisclosed states should query each portal's own interface or file a transparency request, both of which are viable given that every state except Durango exposes at least a searchable or listable endpoint.

## 7. Conclusions and Recommendations

### 7.1 Conclusions

The inventory confirms full 32-state coverage of online sentence publication in Mexico, with **31 states operating a verifiable online channel and 28 portals confirmed working** as of 18 September 2026. Multi-version states were resolved in every case: the State of Mexico's current system is gestiondocumental.pjedomex.gob.mx, San Luis Potosí's is the v2.3 application on its own subdomain, Jalisco legitimately runs two concurrent official portals split by instance, and Morelos, Chihuahua and Durango each required corrections of outdated or dead URLs. On volume, the only defensible public figures place **Guanajuato (≈326,154) and Nuevo León (≈284,873) at the top**, with the State of Mexico above 166,000 as of late 2022; most states simply do not disclose totals, and the report records that fact rather than guessing.

### 7.2 Recommendations

For any operational use of this inventory, four actions follow directly. First, re-verify Veracruz, San Luis Potosí and Puebla from a Mexican IP address, since all three show perimeter filtering rather than genuine outages. Second, flag Tlaxcala's expired certificate to the tribunal's webmaster — it is a five-minute fix that currently renders the state's entire sentence consultation inaccessible. Third, for states whose counts matter, prefer portals with live counters (Guanajuato, Nuevo León, Baja California Sur) as reliable monitoring sources, and treat official-report figures for the State of Mexico, Jalisco and Zacatecas as dated lower bounds. Fourth, treat Durango as a transparency-request case: it is the only state where the answer to "where are the sentences" is "in yearly SIPOT folders" rather than a searchable system. This report's master table (Section 3.1) is the authoritative checklist for coverage, and all 32 rows were verified individually rather than assumed.
